The defining narrative of the trading day was undeniably the Bank of England’s decision to raise the base rate to 4.0%, marking the first increase in over three years. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 8-1 to implement the 25 basis point hike, a move that was widely anticipated but delivered with a notably hawkish tone. Governor Andrew Bailey addressed concerns that the central bank had moved too late, asserting that the UK economy is running hotter than previously forecast, driven significantly by robust business investment and the rapid expansion of the AI sector. This acknowledgment of AI as a primary growth engine is a critical signal for our technology and semiconductor clients, suggesting that the current capital expenditure cycle is not merely a speculative bubble but a structural shift in productivity. However, the market reaction was mixed; while the hike was priced in, the accompanying commentary on risk premia rather than pure rate expectations kept gilt yields volatile, with 5-year Treasuries hovering just below 4.10%.

In the currency markets, sterling reached its highest value against a basket of currencies since the Brexit vote, reflecting a combination of the rate hike and broader global sentiment. The pound’s strength against the dollar was initially bolstered by the BoE decision, though it faced headwinds from a stronger US dollar driven by hawkish Federal Reserve signals and robust US jobs data. This divergence highlights the complex interplay between UK domestic policy and global macro trends. For institutional investors, the strength of the pound may pressure the earnings of UK-listed exporters, particularly in the energy and defence sectors, where a significant portion of revenue is generated in dollars or euros. The FTSE 100, however, remained resilient, nearing record highs as markets digested the political stability following recent US midterm elections, which saw Democrats take control of Congress without significant escalation in geopolitical tensions.

On the corporate front, the day saw notable activity in the financial technology and rare earths sectors. Airtel Money, an African fintech giant, began conditional trading on the London main market as part of its £5.3 billion IPO, signaling continued investor appetite for high-growth tech assets despite the rising rate environment. Simultaneously, Mkango Magnetic Materials Limited announced the effectiveness of its Form F-4 registration statement, a key step in its corporate restructuring and potential US listing. This move is significant for the rare earths and semiconductor supply chain, as Mkango’s operations in critical minerals are increasingly viewed as strategic assets in the global race for technological independence. The success of these listings suggests that while liquidity is tightening, capital is still flowing into sectors with strong long-term structural tailwinds, particularly those aligned with energy transition and tech sovereignty.

Looking at the broader market structure, we are witnessing a post-midterm rotation that is spreading globally, with chip stocks sliding as investors seek safety in broader equities. This rotation underscores a shift in risk appetite, where the initial euphoria around AI-driven growth is being tempered by valuation concerns and the reality of a higher-for-longer interest rate regime. The energy sector also saw movement, with soaring gas costs squeezing out negative power prices in Europe, a dynamic that benefits UK wind farm operators but poses challenges for traditional thermal generators. As we look ahead, the key question is whether the BoE’s hawkish stance will dampen the AI investment cycle or merely slow its pace. Investors should watch the next few weeks for signs of whether the current rate environment will trigger a broader de-risking in technology and semiconductor equities, or if the structural demand for these assets will prove resilient enough to absorb the higher cost of capital.
